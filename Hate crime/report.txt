================================================================================
HATE CRIME INCIDENT DATA — PROFESSIONAL ANALYSIS REPORT
================================================================================

1. DATASET OVERVIEW
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Source file:        hate_crime.csv
Records:             71 hate-crime incidents (Tempe, AZ area), spanning 2010-2023
Original columns:    23
Business context:    Each row is one bias-motivated crime report, including the
                      motivating bias type, offense category, location type,
                      victim characteristics, date, and geocoordinates.

Inferred target for machine learning: "Bias Category" — a 3-class simplification
of the fine-grained "Type of Bias" field, grouping incidents into:
  - Race/Ethnicity
  - Religion
  - Sexual Orientation / Gender Identity
This simplification was necessary because the raw "Type of Bias" field has 8
distinct labels, several with only 1-2 examples — too sparse to model reliably
in a 71-row dataset.

2. DATA CLEANING SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Dropped administrative/redundant columns: OBJECTID, duplicate X/Y and x/y
  coordinate projections, RMS ID, Created On, Last Edited On, Date of Status
  Change (100% missing), and Notes on Crime (97% missing / not informative).
- Recovered ~35 of 43 missing "Type of Bias" values using the fully-populated
  parallel numeric bias code column ("Type of Bias.1"), cutting true target
  missingness from 60% to ~11%. The numeric code column was then dropped to
  avoid duplicating the target.
- Standardized inconsistent category spellings (e.g. "Church" vs.
  "Church/Synagogue/Temple/Mosque"; "Street" vs. "Highway/Road/Street"),
  and consolidated equivalent gender/race labels.
- Extracted Year, Month, and Day-of-Week from the raw crime date, then dropped
  the raw date string.
- Filled 1 missing "Count of Victims" value with the column median (robust to
  the right-skewed distribution).
- Explicitly encoded missing "Type of Victim", "Race of Victims", and "Gender
  of Victim(s)" as an "Unknown" category rather than dropping these columns or
  rows outright — preserving all 71 already-scarce records.
- Collapsed rare offense/location categories (<3 occurrences) into "Other" to
  reduce noise from high-cardinality, low-frequency labels.
- No duplicate rows were found.
- No missing values remain in the final cleaned dataset.

3. EXPLORATORY DATA ANALYSIS — KEY FINDINGS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Offense mix is dominated by Criminal Damage (15), Assault (10), and
  Disorderly Conduct (8); multi-victim incidents cluster in a small subset of
  offense types (mainly assault and criminal-damage variants).
- Count of Victims is right-skewed: most incidents involve a single victim,
  with a long tail up to 8.
- "Early Case Closure" is the single most common case outcome (24 of 71),
  ahead of "Active" (16) and "Inactive" (14).
- Residence/Home is the most common incident location (23 of 71), followed by
  Highway/Road/Street and Church/Synagogue/Temple/Mosque venues (6 each).
- Yearly incident counts fluctuate without a clear long-term trend; given the
  small total sample, year-over-year swings should not be over-interpreted.
- Numeric features (victim count, latitude, longitude) show negligible
  pairwise correlation — no meaningful multicollinearity present.

4. STATISTICAL FINDINGS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Count of Victims shows positive (right) skew, consistent with a small number
  of higher-victim-count outlier incidents; these were retained as legitimate
  events rather than treated as data errors.
- A full VIF/multicollinearity table was not warranted: with only 3 numeric
  features and near-zero pairwise correlation, no redundancy was detected.

5. FEATURE ENGINEERING SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Target engineered: "Bias Category" (3-class grouping of "Type of Bias").
- Datetime features extracted: Crime Year, Crime Month, Crime Day of Week.
- Categorical standardization and rare-category collapsing applied to
  "Type of Location" and "Type of Offense/Crime".
- Missingness in categorical victim/race/gender fields encoded as an explicit
  "Unknown" level, preserving the signal that "not recorded" is itself
  informative rather than imputing a possibly-incorrect majority class.
- Final ML-ready file drops near-unique, identifier-like text ("Obfuscated
  Address of Crime") and the raw date string, since their information content
  is already captured by geocoordinates and the extracted date parts.

6. MACHINE LEARNING RESULTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Task:                Multiclass classification of Bias Category
Usable labeled rows:  63 (8 rows with unresolved/"Unknown" bias code excluded)
Split:                75% train / 25% test, stratified
Models compared:      Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest,
                       Gradient Boosting, SVM, KNN (5-fold stratified CV,
                       scored on macro-F1)
Best model:            Gradient Boosting Classifier
Tuned hyperparameters: learning_rate=0.05, n_estimators=200
Cross-validated F1-macro (train): ~0.69
Held-out test accuracy:            0.75
Held-out test F1-macro:            0.72
Top predictive features:           offense type (criminal damage / aggravated
                                    criminal damage / non-DV assault), whether
                                    victim race was recorded, crime month,
                                    and geolocation.

7. BUSINESS INSIGHTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Residences are the leading incident location, suggesting community/victim
  outreach after a bias-motivated crime should have a strong home-visit or
  neighborhood-safety component, not only public-space patrolling.
- The high "Early Case Closure" rate alongside "Active"/"Inactive" statuses
  points to potential value in reviewing case-handling consistency and
  clearance-criteria documentation across bias-crime reports.
- Offense type is the strongest available signal for the type of bias
  motivating an incident, which could support automatic triage/flagging of
  incoming reports for the appropriate specialized unit (e.g. civil-rights,
  religious-hate-crime liaison) once more training data is available.

8. RECOMMENDATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Treat the trained classifier as a proof-of-concept only; do not use it for
   operational triage without validating on a substantially larger, more
   recent dataset (n well into the hundreds).
2. Improve data capture at intake: victim race, victim type, and gender are
   missing 40-66% of the time, which limits both analysis and modeling.
3. Standardize categorical value entry at the source (several near-duplicate
   labels for the same location/gender concept were found and had to be
   manually reconciled).
4. Periodically re-run this pipeline as new incidents are logged, to monitor
   whether bias-category mix or location patterns shift over time.

9. FINAL CONCLUSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This dataset documents 71 bias-motivated crimes with rich categorical and
geographic detail, but very limited volume. After cleaning, imputing the
target from a redundant numeric code, and engineering a coarser 3-class bias
category, a Gradient Boosting classifier trained on offense type, location,
victim characteristics, and geography achieved a test macro-F1 of ~0.72 —
promising for a first pass, but not something to deploy operationally without
substantially more data. The accompanying notebooks (analysis.ipynb,
ML_pipeline.ipynb), cleaned dataset (ready_for_ML_model.csv), and serialized
pipeline (model.pkl) provide a complete, reproducible foundation for future
iteration as more incidents are recorded.
